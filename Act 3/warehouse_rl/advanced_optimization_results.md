## Reflection 
My goal was to improve staffing and package pickup while building on my existing warehouse layout strategy. In the initial 10-episode benchmark, my agent ranked first out of eight with an average profit of $33,563.40, a reported 95% confidence interval of ±$2,744.18, and a 68.6% order completion rate. My first changes reduced performance, showing that more structured decision rules do not automatically produce better results. I revised the hiring thresholds and shortened staffing reviews from every 25 steps to every 5 steps so the agent could respond sooner to waiting orders. The final version maintained first place with an average profit of $33,654.17 and a reported confidence interval of ±$2,622.12. Profit increased by $90.77 over baseline, while completion decreased slightly to 68.4%. These results indicate comparable overall performance rather than a statistically established improvement. However, the final version recovered $3,936.98 in average profit compared with the preceding version. This process taught me to evaluate changes against my own baseline rather than assume that finishing first means every modification was successful.



I replaced random staffing decisions with rules that consider waiting orders, worker availability, and warehouse profit. The agent hires when demand is high and most workers are busy, respects the employee limit, and dismisses an idle worker only when there is spare capacity and a reason to reduce wage costs. It preserves at least two workers, protects employees completing orders, and hires a missing manager without waiting for positive profit. For package pickup, I replaced random assignments with a greedy strategy that compares available worker–order pairs using pickup proximity and order value, choosing the highest-scoring pairs without assigning a worker or order twice. These algorithms support one another. For example, keeping a manager available allows a frequently retrieved product to be moved closer to delivery. The pickup algorithm then uses that product’s updated location when matching workers to orders, while demand-based/iring provides capacity to handle waiting orders. Together, these decisions can reduce travel and delays, but excessive hiring adds wages and may increase congestion. Their value depends on whether additional completed orders justify operating costs. One limitation is that the pickup score considers only the nearest required item rather than the entire order’s route. I would next estimate complete pickup travel time and order urgency, then compare versions using matched random seeds to better separate algorithm effects from simulation variability.
